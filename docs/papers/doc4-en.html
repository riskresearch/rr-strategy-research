Housing as Collateral

The financing capacity of housing as a wealth advantage.

1.Purpose and framing

In the wealth-architecture document, investment real estate is classified as a satellite asset: it concentrates in a single, illiquid, indivisible good what an index spreads across thousands of companies, and it bears the heaviest taxation and the highest transaction costs of all asset classes. That analysis is correct when housing is assessed as a source of return. This document examines a different dimension, which that assessment leaves out and in which housing holds a singular position: its capacity to serve as financing collateral.

The framing can be stated as a comparative question. Among the assets accessible to an individual investor, none allows financing for so high a fraction of its value, over such long terms and on such stable conditions, as residential property. That property, and not the asset's expected return, is the economic reason housing retains a place of its own in wealth planning. The document quantifies the cost of ownership, examines the financing capacity that distinguishes it, compares direct investment with listed vehicles, and describes how mortgages are granted and structured in practice.

2.The cost of property ownership

The trade-off of real-estate investment is substantial and must be quantified precisely. Acquiring and holding a property carries costs a financial asset does not bear: the transfer tax, which ranges from 6% to 11% on a resale purchase, or VAT on new build; notary, registry, and administration fees; the annual property tax; maintenance costs and community levies; and, on sale, a fresh taxation of the gain and the municipal capital-gains tax. The combined entry and exit cost of a real-estate transaction frequently runs between 10% and 15% of the property's value, against the few hundredths of a percent of a listed fund. To this are added illiquidity, which may require months to complete a sale, and the indivisibility of the asset.

If the analysis ended here, investment housing would prove inferior to a global index core as an accumulation instrument. The assessment is, however, incomplete, because it omits the asset the property generates and that no other provides to the same degree: a broad, stable, low-cost borrowing capacity.

3.Financing capacity as an asset

The loan-to-value (LTV) expresses the fraction of an asset's value that can be financed with debt secured by it. It is the metric that reveals the asymmetry between housing and other asset classes: residential property admits the highest LTV accessible to an individual and, unlike the rest, its financing is not revised downward when the asset's price declines.

An ordinary residential mortgage finances around 80% of the appraised value, and certain structures —examined in section 6— allow reaching up to 100% of the purchase price. A quality fixed-income portfolio admits a secured credit that may approach 90% of its value; a portfolio of stocks or funds, around 50% to 60%. These latter two, however, present a decisive difference from the mortgage: the pledged portfolio is valued daily at market, and if its value falls such that the LTV exceeds the maintenance threshold, the lender may demand additional collateral or liquidate the position. This is the margin call. Mortgage financing has no such mechanism.

4.Direct ownership or listed vehicles

Exposure to the real-estate market admits two routes: the direct acquisition of a property or investment in specialized listed vehicles, such as REITs —in Spain, SOCIMIs—. Both offer exposure to the same asset class, but they differ in respects worth contrasting neutrally, because the choice depends on the investor's objective.

The listed vehicle provides immediate liquidity, diversification across numerous properties, divisibility, and professional management, without the operational burdens of direct ownership. These advantages are real, but it is worth examining why, despite investing in the same underlying asset, the REIT does not reproduce the properties that make direct housing attractive. Four structural reasons converge.

The first is tax. The regime that defines these vehicles —the REIT in its international form, the SOCIMI in Spain— requires them to distribute most of their profits each year in order to obtain the corporate-tax exemption. That mandatory distribution translates into a dividend the investor receives and is taxed on annually, so there is no possibility of deferring the gain by accumulating it within the vehicle. This forgoes the mechanism that, in direct investment held long-term, allows appreciation to compound untaxed until sale.

The second is the absence of the investor's own leverage, which is the central point of this document. The REIT leverages its own balance sheet, at the corporate level, but the holder does not obtain, by owning its shares, a financing capacity different from that of any other listed security: its shares are pledged at a moderate LTV, on the order of 50% to 60%, and are subject to margin call. Direct housing, by contrast, confers on the investor access to a mortgage of 80% to 100% with no daily mark-to-market. The REIT investor receives real-estate rents; the direct owner receives real-estate rents and, in addition, the financing capacity no other asset matches.

The third is market behavior. Although its underlying asset is property, the REIT trades on an exchange and behaves like equity: its volatility is comparable to that of the stock index, and its correlation with it rises precisely in episodes of stress, when diversification would be most valuable. The price of direct housing, not being valued daily, does not transmit that volatility to the owner's wealth and cannot trigger forced sales.

The fourth is the double layer of costs and agency. The REIT investor bears the vehicle's management costs and depends on the decisions of its managers —corporate leverage, acquisition policy, capital increases that may dilute the holder— over which they have no control. The direct owner assumes the property's operating costs but retains full control of the operation and its financing.

The conclusion is not that the REIT is a poor instrument: to obtain liquid, diversified exposure to the real-estate sector, without operational management, it is more efficient than buying a flat. But it does not reproduce the property that motivates direct investment in housing —a high, stable leverage capacity immune to the margin call— and for that reason the two instruments serve different objectives and are not substitutes. For pure real-estate exposure, the listed vehicle is preferable; for stable financing capacity, direct housing has no alternative.

5.How a mortgage is granted

Mortgage lending rests on three criteria that institutions assess jointly. The first is repayment capacity, measured chiefly by the effort ratio, that is, the proportion of net monthly income devoted to servicing the debt; as a general reference, institutions tend to set the prudential limit at around 30%–35% of income. The second is the LTV, the ratio between the loan amount and the lower of two values, the appraised value and the purchase price; the standard sits at 80% for a primary residence, and below that for a second home or investment. The third is the borrower's profile: the stability and source of income, credit history, accumulated savings, and additional guarantees available.

The resulting payment depends on the principal, the term, and the interest rate applied. Lengthening the term reduces the monthly payment but increases the total interest cost; the relationship between the two is not linear, so the first years of additional term reduce the payment appreciably, while the last barely change it. The institution grants the loan when the three criteria are satisfied simultaneously: a low LTV does not offset insufficient repayment capacity, nor the reverse.

6.Financing the whole operation

The standard mortgage finances around 80% of value, so the complete operation requires covering the remaining 20% and the purchase costs, which together amount to roughly 30% of the property's value. How that mortgage-unfinanced fraction is covered determines whether the operation is viable without disbursing one's own savings.

Structures exist that allow financing the whole operation, or the part not covered by the main mortgage. Among the common ones are the provision of a second guarantee —another unencumbered property, one's own or a relative's, that expands the collateral and raises the effective LTV—; the combination of products, in which a complementary loan covers the down payment and costs; and certain operations on bank-owned properties, in which the institution finances percentages above the standard. Each of these routes increases total borrowing and therefore risk, and its viability depends closely on the borrower's repayment capacity, which remains the determining criterion.

7.The investment: rents and appreciation

The economic logic of investment housing is organized in two phases. During the holding period, the property is let, and the rent received services the mortgage; to the extent that the net rent covers the payment, the tenant in effect finances the amortization of the loan, and the investor's net worth grows as the outstanding debt is reduced. At the end of the horizon, the sale of the property realizes the accumulated appreciation in value, once the remaining debt is cancelled. The return on the operation thus combines the progressive reduction of the liability financed by rents and the revaluation of the asset.

Leverage amplifies both effects. Since the mortgage finances most of the price, the property's appreciation is computed on the total value, while the capital actually contributed by the investor is a fraction of it; the return on equity is consequently higher than the property's revaluation. This amplification operates in both directions: a fall in value reduces net worth in the same magnified proportion, though the absence of a margin call prevents that decline from forcing an early sale as long as the payment is met. The operation is, by its nature, long-term, and its outcome depends on rents covering the debt service in a sustained way over the entire horizon.

8.Synthesis

The balanced judgment brings together the two sides of the asset. Housing bears the heaviest taxation and the highest transaction costs of all asset classes, and as an accumulation instrument it does not compete with a global index core. It is, at the same time, the best collateral available to an individual investor: the highest LTV, the most stable long-term financing among secured debt, and the only one immune to the margin call. Both statements are simultaneously true, and the apparent contradiction resolves by distinguishing functions: housing is not an efficient return engine, but it constitutes a first-order wealth foundation.

Its place in the wealth architecture follows from this. The primary residence covers the real liability that future rent represents, fixing its cost with the most stable financing available. Investment housing, acquired with leverage and let out, allows rents to amortize the debt and aims at the asset's long-term appreciation. And the borrowing capacity the property generates, used with the prudent-LTV discipline that governs credit on the financial core, constitutes a source of structural liquidity that does not require selling productive assets. The concrete design of these operations —the prudent LTV in each case, the combination of products that makes it possible, and its fit with the rest of the wealth— belongs to the individual analysis of each situation.
