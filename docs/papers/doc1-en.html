Capital and Time

Foundations of long-term investing: why cash loses value systematically, which assets protect purchasing power, and how to build an efficient, evidence-based portfolio that is sustainable over decades

1.Purpose and scope

This document sets out the foundations of long-term investing for the saver who suspects — correctly — that holding wealth in bank deposits does not constitute a preservation strategy. It presumes no prior financial training: each concept is defined where it appears, and technical terms are linked to reference sources for independent verification. Every claim rests on public data with more than a century of history, cited at the end.

It is worth also delimiting what this document does not contain: neither methods of accelerated enrichment, nor forecasts of short-term market behavior — which no participant demonstrably possesses. Its object is more modest and more useful: to identify the few structural decisions that, sustained over time, determine the difference between building wealth over a lifetime and watching it erode.

2.The risk you don't see

The first question is what exactly a checking account or a deposit protects. The answer is: the nominal amount — €100,000 will still be €100,000 twenty years from now. What it does not protect is what that amount can buy: its purchasing power. The distinction between nominal value and real value is the foundation on which all subsequent analysis is built.

Prices rise systematically, and this is not an accidental phenomenon: it is a declared objective of monetary policy. Central banks — the ECB in Europe, the Federal Reserve in the U.S. — set an official target of 2% annual inflation. A rate that may seem modest implies, applied on a compound basis, that cash loses half its purchasing power in about 35 years.

Why central banks set a positive inflation target

That the target is 2% — and not 0% — is not arbitrary, and understanding it clarifies why the erosion of cash is a permanent feature of the system rather than a passing anomaly. Four reasons converge. The first is the margin against deflation: a sustained fall in prices leads households and firms to postpone consumption and investment in anticipation of lower prices, which depresses activity and can feed on itself — as in Japan during the 1990s and 2000s; a cushion of positive inflation keeps the economy away from that trap. The second is room for monetary policy: central banks stimulate the economy by lowering their interest rate, but that rate cannot be cut much below zero; with a 2% inflation target, nominal rates in normal times sit higher, which leaves room to cut them in a recession. The third is the downward rigidity of wages: cutting a nominal wage is socially and contractually very difficult, so moderate inflation allows real wages to adjust — when they must — without explicit nominal cuts, facilitating the adjustment of the labor market. The fourth, less openly stated but historically decisive, is public-debt management: a heavily indebted state sees inflation erode the real value of its debt, and keeping interest rates below inflation — financial repression — transfers wealth from the saver in cash and bonds to the sovereign debtor; this is how the United States and the United Kingdom reduced the real weight of their postwar debt between 1945 and 1980. For the investor, the operational conclusion of all four reasons is the same: positive inflation is not an accident that will be corrected, but a policy objective best taken as given — and one from which only real assets, not cash, provide protection.

It is worth emphasizing, moreover, that 2% is the favorable scenario — the one in which the target is met: in Spain, prices grew at an average above 13% per year during the 1970s and 1980s; in 2022 they again exceeded 10% in a single year; and between 2021 and 2023 the euro-area price level accumulated an increase of nearly 18%. Anyone who held their wealth in an account during that three-year span lost around a fifth of their real wealth without the nominal balance ever registering a decline.

The right panel translates those averages into the purchasing power of €100 held in cash over 30 years: the result falls between €54 and €33 by region — a loss of between 46% and 67% of real wealth, gradual, silent, and of very high probability.

The central idea Over a horizon of decades, the risk-free alternative does not exist. There are two risks to choose between: the visible volatility of markets, which fluctuates but is rewarded, or the invisible erosion of cash, which does not fluctuate and loses value almost systematically. The pertinent question is not how to avoid the fluctuations, but which assets protect and grow purchasing power — and how to hold them efficiently.

3.The evidence of 125 years

The question of which assets protect purchasing power requires no conjecture: a systematic record exists of the returns of the main asset classes across 35 countries since 1900 — the Global Investment Returns Yearbook. Its conclusion, measured in real return (net of inflation), is decisive: equities have returned around 5.2% per year above inflation worldwide over 125 years. Bonds, around 1.7%. Short-term Treasury bills, 0.5% — and the unremunerated cash of a checking account, below even that figure: it literally generates nothing, and loses year after year all the purchasing power inflation takes. Gold, under 1%. House prices, around 1% since 1950.

The explanation for this premium is structural. A share is a participation in the ownership of a company, and a company is a productive asset with the capacity to adapt: it adjusts its prices when costs rise, reallocates resources from failing lines to prospering ones, and reinvests part of its earnings each year. No other asset in the comparison — a fixed-coupon bond, an ounce of gold, a bank balance — has equivalent mechanisms. Equities are therefore, structurally, the asset class that participates in the growth of the economy, and the only one that has consistently outpaced inflation for more than a century.

The right panel of the figure also documents the dispersion of returns across national markets. U.S. equities have returned 6.6% real per year since 1900; the world index, 5.2%; European equities, 4.4%; and Spanish equities, 3.5%. The historical dispersion is wider still across the full set of countries: some markets have delivered returns well above the average over extended periods, while others have recorded sustained real losses, and several fully extinguished their investors' capital following wars or institutional ruptures. Identifying which is which in advance is not reliably feasible. Concentrating wealth in one's home market, known as home bias, means bearing that idiosyncratic risk without an expected compensation for it: it has cost the Spanish investor, on average, more than one and a half real points per year against the world index. It follows that exposure should be global; a world index incorporates every market and requires no anticipation of which will lead.

A difference of three or four points per year may seem minor; compound interest — growth applied each year on an already-increased base — makes it decisive:

Over a 50-year horizon, each euro invested in world equities has multiplied by more than 12 in real terms; in bonds, by 2.3; in cash, it has barely preserved its value. Time, which erodes idle money, is the principal asset of invested money. This premium has, however, a counterpart that must be stated precisely: equities record drawdowns of 30% to 50% once or twice per decade, and have gone through periods of more than ten years without net advance. The investor who liquidates positions during those phases does not receive the premium; it is received by those who have the structure — of liquidity and of discipline — needed to hold them. The rest of this document is built to equip the reader with that structure.

4.Implementation: index investing

With global equities established as the destination of the investment, the next question is the method: select individual securities, delegate that selection to a professional manager, or replicate the market as a whole? This decision also requires no conjecture: it is systematically measured. Since 2002, S&P Dow Jones has published the SPIVA study, which compares each actively managed fund with its category index — the index being the weighted average of the entire market, replicable today automatically and at minimal cost through an index fund or ETF. The result is consistent across every region and period: over ten or fifteen years, around nine out of ten professionally managed funds underperform the index they seek to beat.

This result does not reflect a lack of professional competence, but an arithmetic constraint, formalized by William Sharpe in "The Arithmetic of Active Management": the set of all investors constitutes the market, so, before costs, aggregate active management earns exactly the index return; after costs, it necessarily falls below. It might be asked whether one can select in advance the group of managers who do outperform the index. The only observable criterion for doing so is past performance, and S&P Dow Jones's persistence studies indicate that it has no predictive value: the proportion of funds that, after ranking among the best in one period, repeat in the next does not exceed what would be expected by chance. To this is added an asymmetry in magnitudes. Managers who beat the index tend to do so by a narrow margin, on the order of one or two points per year, while those who fall short, the majority, do so by larger margins. The mathematical expectation of active selection is therefore unfavorable.

The effect of fees

A third factor erodes performance: management fees. Unlike an ordinary expense, they are not paid separately but are deducted each year from the invested capital, regardless of the fund's outcome. An annual fee of 1% or 2% appears modest, but its effect compounds over time with the same mechanics as return: each amount deducted in one year no longer compounds in every subsequent year. The pertinent measure of its impact is not the annual percentage, but the fraction of final capital it absorbs over the whole investment:

The right panel deserves a careful reading: with identical market return, a 2% annual fee absorbs 43% of final capital over 30 years — nearly half of future wealth, in exchange for a service that, as SPIVA shows, underperforms on average the alternative costing 0.1%. The evidence recommends, in general terms, low-cost index management, which retains for the investor the fraction of wealth that expensive active management removes in fees.

5.Tax deferral: the value of not selling

The second erosion factor is tax, and its effect depends largely on when the gain is realized. A capital gain is taxed only when it is materialized through a sale; as long as the investment is held, the accumulated gain generates no tax liability. The deferred tax on a latent gain therefore remains invested and compounding in the investor's favor until the moment of sale. Each early realization brings that payment forward and permanently reduces the base on which compounding operates. The difference over thirty years is considerable:

An investor who realizes gains every year turns €1 into €4.55; one who holds for decades without selling turns it into more than €7. The market return is identical: the difference comes exclusively from the tax calendar. Two practical decisions follow. First: select the accumulating class of the fund — the one that reinvests dividends automatically within the product, with no annual taxation. Second: invest with the intention of holding for decades. The apparent inactivity of the long-term investor is, measured in net outcome, the most efficient tax policy available.

6.The portfolio, in practice

The preceding sections converge on a portfolio of a simplicity that may surprise — until the evidence supporting it is reviewed. It has two components, constituted in this order:

First component: the liquidity reserve. Between 6 and 18 months of household spending, depending on each household's circumstances, for contingencies and day-to-day financial stability. Not in a checking account at 0%, but in Treasury bills or a money market fund, which pay the prevailing interest rate at minimal risk. The reserve also serves a strategic function: it is what allows the core not to be liquidated during market drawdowns.

Second component: the investment core. The rest of the capital, invested in a single type of product: a global equity index ETF, in its accumulating class, at low total cost, fed by automatic periodic contributions (the technique known as dollar-cost averaging) and held for decades. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is an investment fund that trades on an exchange like a stock, with intraday liquidity and a low management cost. In its accumulating class it reinvests dividends within the fund itself, with no distribution and no annual taxation for the holder. As a practical criterion, select the class denominated in the currency in which income is received and withdrawals will be made —the euro, for most investors resident in Spain—, in order to avoid conversion costs on contributions and redemptions.

Where you buy it matters almost as much as what you buy: the trading and custody fees of many traditional banks erode savings the same way management fees do (section 5). A low-cost broker — Interactive Brokers is a common reference for fees, market breadth, and strength — reduces that cost to a minimal fraction. Each element of this specification comes from a section of this document: equities, from section 3; global, from the analysis of home bias; indexed and low-cost, from section 4; accumulating and held long-term, from section 5.

As for timing of entry: the evidence on predicting short-term market levels is consistently negative, and automatic periodic contribution resolves the question by distributing purchases across all moments of the cycle and eliminating the discretionary decision.

The complete plan, in four points 1 · Reserve of 6–18 months of spending in Treasury bills or a money market fund.
2 · The rest of the capital, in a global accumulating index ETF (cost < 0.25%).
3 · Automatic monthly contribution.
4 · Discipline: annual review; in the face of market drawdowns — which will occur — hold positions and contributions per section 3.

A note on the horizon. The policy of holding without selling applies during the accumulation phase, when the capital has no near-term use date. As the investor ages and the moment to draw on the wealth approaches, the natural course is to realize positions gradually, but only to the extent the money is needed: a sale is justified by the need to spend, not by a market forecast. Selling everything at once to "secure" what has been accumulated reintroduces the timing risk that periodic contribution had removed, and needlessly brings forward the taxation of the gain (section 5). The criterion, therefore, is to liquidate what will be spent, when it will be spent, and to keep the rest invested.
