Wealth Architecture

Structure, taxation, and financing of long-term capital: how to organize a wealth of some size around a global equity core — what role to assign each asset class, how to optimize the tax burden, and how to obtain liquidity without interrupting compounding

1.Purpose and scope

This document is addressed to the investor who already knows the foundations of long-term investing, developed in our foundations document, and whose wealth situation presents dimensions beyond the basic portfolio: a larger capital, real-estate assets, liquidity needs, or estate planning. It examines, on comparable terms, the full set of asset classes, the taxation that determines how to hold each one, the specification of the core, the obtaining of liquidity through credit on the portfolio, and the available financing structures. The claims rest on the public sources cited at the end; tax and market parameters are presented as indicative ranges subject to professional verification.

2.Overview of asset classes

The starting point is an inventory of the asset classes evaluated by homogeneous criteria. Each asset class is examined here in the dimensions that determine the long-run outcome: its structural role (foundation or satellite), the nature of its return (an asset without cash flow does not compound internally), its historical real return in long samples — also expressed as a multiplier per decade — its taxation, its measurable frictions, and its net advantages and disadvantages. The cards — expandable — are organized by proximity to the core role: first listed productive assets, then real assets, next private markets, and finally speculative ones.

Listed productive assets

Listed equities (stocks)
Structural role
The candidate foundational layer: fractional ownership of thousands of productive companies across every sector and geography.
Cash flow or growth
Both: reinvested earnings (growth) and dividends. Corporate revenues reprice with the nominal economy — the structural answer to inflation.
Historical real return
≈5.2% p.a. for the world index 1900–2024 (≈×1.66 per decade); U.S. ≈6.6% (≈×1.90 per decade).
Taxation
The most malleable of all: with the right vehicle (an accumulating fund/ETF), nothing is taxed while unsold — see section 3.
Frictions and constraints
Minimal: costs of a few basis points (TER 0.10–0.25% in global ETFs), liquidity in seconds, divisible to the euro.
Advantages
Highest long-run real premium; instant diversification; no operational management; universal access with no minimums.
Disadvantages
Visible volatility: 30–50% drawdowns occur every decade and must be endured without selling; flat stretches of 10–15 years have existed (e.g. 2000–2013). The risk is real — but it is the only reliably rewarded one. See risk premium.
Government bonds
Structural role
Ballast and liability matching; liquidity reserve in short maturities (bills).
Cash flow or growth
Fixed nominal coupon — precisely why inflation is its weak point.
Historical real return
≈1.7% p.a. for the world aggregate 1900–2024 (≈×1.18 per decade); bills, ≈0.5%.
Taxation
Coupons taxed annually as investment income; no accumulation mechanism except via funds.
Frictions and constraints
Duration risk: in 2022 the global aggregate fell ~−16% nominal — its worst year in a century — as rates rose amid high inflation.
Advantages
A legitimate portfolio stabilizer; very short maturities are the correct instrument for the liquidity reserve (section 7).
Disadvantages
Structurally incapable of being the engine: its long-run real return does not meet compounding objectives, and it fails against inflation exactly when most needed.
Corporate bonds
Structural role
Income satellite: the rung between government bonds and equities.
Cash flow or growth
Government coupon + credit spread (historically ~0.5–1% net of defaults in investment grade; more in high yield, with more defaults).
Historical real return
≈0.5–1 point p.a. over the comparable government bond over the very long run, net of default losses.
Taxation
Like government bonds: coupon taxed annually.
Frictions and constraints
Liquidity below that of government bonds; the spread widens in crises.
Advantages
More income than government bonds with bounded risk in investment grade; diversifies the source of return.
Disadvantages
Its credit risk behaves like diluted equity: it falls with the stock market under stress, adding less diversification than it appears. High yield, in particular, is half equity with bond taxation.

Real assets

Direct real estate
Structural role
The primary residence: consumption + forced saving + rent hedge. As an investment, a concentrated satellite.
Cash flow or growth
Rental income (gross 3–6%) plus real price appreciation ≈1% p.a. since 1950 (Knoll et al.); before 1950, ≈0% real.
Historical real return
Price: ≈×1.12 per decade. Total return with rents is higher gross (Jordà et al.), but computed before taxes, capex ~1–2% p.a., vacancy, and management.
Taxation
The class with the most layers: transfer tax on entry (6–11% in Spain), rental income taxed each year at the general scale (marginals up to ~47–54%), annual property tax, and the sale taxed again.
Frictions and constraints
Round trip ≈10–15% of value in Spain (2–6% in the U.S./northern Europe); months to sell; indivisible; one asset, one city.
Advantages
The primary residence has economic sense of its own: in many markets the rent saved exceeds the equivalent mortgage payment, so buying the home one will live in is hedging a real liability (future rent) with non-callable fixed-rate financing. Tangible; leverageable with a mortgage.
Disadvantages
As an additional investment, it concentrates in a single asset what an index spreads across thousands, with the worst taxation and worst liquidity on the list, and a personal management burden that grows over time.
REITs (listed real-estate vehicles)
Structural role
Sector satellite: real-estate exposure with exchange liquidity.
Cash flow or growth
High mandatory dividend distribution (REIT regime), little retained growth.
Historical real return
Comparable to equities over the very long run, with more regime variance.
Taxation
Unfavorable by design: being required to distribute, they generate unavoidable annual dividend taxation — there is no accumulating class.
Frictions and constraints
Structurally leveraged; sensitive to the rate cycle: in 2022 global REIT indices fell around −25%, at the same time as equities and bonds.
Advantages
Liquidity and diversification across properties impossible in direct real estate; professional management.
Disadvantages
A single capital-intensive sector (~2–3% of the world index); high correlation with equities precisely in crises; the forced dividend destroys tax deferral.
Gold and commodities
Structural role
Hedge satellite against extreme monetary and geopolitical scenarios.
Cash flow or growth
None: no coupon, no earnings, no rent. Only price.
Historical real return
<1% p.a. for gold over the very long run (≈×1.07 per decade), with real drawdowns spanning two decades (1980–2000).
Taxation
The sale with a gain is taxed as savings income; physical and ETC vehicles add custody costs.
Frictions and constraints
High volatility for a yieldless asset; commodity futures bear roll costs.
Advantages
Decorrelation in monetary crises; centuries of universal acceptance.
Disadvantages
Without cash flow there is no internal compounding: it is insurance, not an engine. Its correct place is tactical and risk-sized.

Private markets

Private equity (buyouts)
Structural role
Institutional satellite: unlisted equity with leverage and lock-up.
Cash flow or growth
Irregular distributions after a J-curve of years; the underlying is corporate earnings, as in listed equity.
Historical real return
Net of fees, post-2006 fund generations return on average ≈ the same as the equivalent listed index (PME ≈1.0–1.1; Harris–Jenkinson–Kaplan). The excess lives in the top quartile.
Taxation
Depends on the vehicle; the lock-up imposes its own realization calendar.
Frictions and constraints
Illiquidity of 10–12 years; «2 and 20» fees with a total effective cost of 3–6% p.a. (Phalippou); high minimums; apparent volatility is low because valued by appraisal, not by lower risk.
Advantages
Access to unlisted companies; the best managers show some persistence.
Disadvantages
The median fund does not compensate for illiquidity and fees; the top quartile rations access to new capital. Without demonstrable access to it, the expected position is «index minus fees, with a decade-long lock-up».
Venture capital
Structural role
Long-tail satellite: optionality on early-stage innovation.
Cash flow or growth
None for years; the return depends on a few extreme exits — a power-law distribution.
Historical real return
The median fund has underperformed the listed index in most fund generations (Kauffman; median PME <1); the top decile concentrates almost all of the excess.
Taxation
Like private equity: dictated by the vehicle and the exit calendar.
Frictions and constraints
A decade-long lock-up; extreme dispersion across funds; access to top-decile managers is closed by excess demand.
Advantages
If — and only if — one accesses the top decile, the return can be extraordinary; strategic and informational value for industrial investors.
Disadvantages
For an investor without that access, the mathematical expectation is below the index with far more illiquidity. The dispersion of the class is not capturable: it belongs to the managers, not the investor.

Speculative assets

Crypto-assets
Structural role
Speculative satellite, if any, and of a size one can afford to lose.
Cash flow or growth
None in the base assets; no earnings or valuation anchor.
Historical real return
Extraordinary and not mechanically repeatable; drawdowns of more than 75% on multiple occasions within the past decade.
Taxation
Gains taxed as savings income in Spain; each swap between coins is a taxable event — ordinary trading destroys deferral.
Frictions and constraints
Custody and platform risk; shifting regulation.
Advantages
Continuous liquidity; optionality on a technological-monetary shift if it occurs.
Disadvantages
Volatility 3–5× that of equities with no flow demonstrably rewarding it; impossible to size as a core with actuarial criteria.

The reading of the synthesis is direct: global listed equities are the only class that combines the highest historical real return with the best liquidity, the lowest cost, the most optimizable taxation, and access without barriers — and are therefore the core. The primary residence has an economic justification of its own: hedging with fixed-rate financing the real liability that future rent constitutes, which in many markets exceeds the equivalent mortgage payment; it is, however, the management of a liability, not the growth engine of wealth. Housing also possesses a property no other asset class matches —its financing capacity, with a high LTV and no margin call—, analyzed in the companion document "Housing as Collateral." Private markets require a verifiable condition — demonstrable access to the top quartile of managers — without which their mathematical expectation is below that of the listed index with far greater illiquidity. Everything else is a satellite, and its inclusion must be justified case by case. It is also worth guarding against a common error: diversification is not an end in itself. Adding asset classes with a lower expected real return merely in order to "diversify" does not reduce the risk relevant to a long-term investor; it lowers the expected return without a clear counterpart. The pertinent diversification is that which occurs within the global core itself —thousands of companies across every sector and country— and, where applicable, that provided by genuinely distinct premia managed with judgment, not the mere accumulation of heterogeneous assets.

3.The comparative taxation of wealth

Taxation alters the final outcome of an investment as much as its gross return, and operates under three principles common to Western jurisdictions. First: deferral has economic value of its own. The tax on an unrealized gain remains invested and compounding in the investor's favor — functionally, cost-free financing from the state that each unnecessary realization cancels prematurely. Second: income is not taxed uniformly. A near-universal hierarchy exists: labor and rental income bear the highest general scales; dividends and interest are taxed annually at intermediate rates; unrealized gains are not taxed; and capital obtained through a loan is not income and is not taxed in any case. Third: inheritance resets the count in several jurisdictions. In the U.S. the step-up in basis applies; in Spain, art. 33.3.b of the Personal Income Tax Law extinguishes the latent gain at death. Where this mechanism exists, the optimal realization policy over the very long run is non-realization.

4.The core and its maintenance

The two preceding sections converge on a practical conclusion: the asset that combines the highest real return with the most favorable taxation is global equity held without selling. From this follows the specification of the core, whose full rationale is developed in the foundations document: a UCITS ETF of global equities — on the FTSE All-World or the MSCI ACWI — in accumulating class, with a total cost below 0.25% per year, fed by periodic contributions and held long-term. Each term answers to evidence: equities, for the long-run real premium; global, for the documented cost of home bias; indexed, for SPIVA's results and the absence of manager persistence; accumulating, for the suppression of annual dividend taxation; held long-term, for the value of deferral culminating in the extinction of the gain at inheritance.

Alongside the core, the liquidity reserve — between 6 and 18 months of spending in Treasury bills or money market funds — serves a dual function: current financial stability and, above all, the behavioral guarantee of not liquidating the core during market drawdowns. The rest of this architecture — credit, financing structures, satellites — is built around these two pieces without altering them.

5.Liquidity without selling: credit on the core

Wealth held long-term needs to solve liquidity by a route other than selling. The structural solution is credit collateralized by the portfolio itself — the strategy known in the U.S. as "buy, borrow, die", fully adaptable to the Spanish framework. Its three steps exploit, one by one, the principles of section 3: buy (the accumulating core, which defers all taxation while held), borrow (a margin credit with the portfolio pledged: the loan is not income and generates no taxable event), and transfer (where the tax basis is reset at death, the latent income-tax gain is extinguished: the heirs do not pay the income tax on the gain accumulated over the owner’s entire life). One point that is often confused deserves precision: this does not mean inheriting tax-free. The transfer is indeed taxed under inheritance and gift tax, which in Spain is regional and taxes the value received — with substantial reliefs for spouse and descendants in several regions (in Catalonia, up to 99% for the spouse). What is extinguished is only the latent income-tax gain, not the inheritance tax; and the outstanding debt on the portfolio reduces the inheritance-tax base, because the heir receives the wealth net of what is owed — that is the sense in which “the estate settles the debt.”

Uses of the credit

Credit secured by the portfolio covers liquidity needs without requiring the core to be sold or the sale to be taxed. Its natural application is liquidity for spending in a broad sense: financing the down payment on a home, the purchase of a vehicle, a renovation, studies, or any personal or unexpected outlay. In these cases, credit secured by the portfolio is usually cheaper than a loan requested from another institution for the same purpose, because the collateral is a liquid portfolio with transparent valuation, which reduces the spread the lender applies. In addition, by not selling the core, the position remains invested and the tax deferral of the gain is preserved.

Credit on the portfolio is not intended to leverage market exposure permanently. That function belongs to the systematic strategies of section 6, which manage exposure with specific instruments and formalized risk control, without turning the core into collateral subject to revision.

The borrowing discipline

The critical parameter is the LTV — debt over portfolio value — because the collateral is marked to market daily and the lender can demand additional collateral (margin call) if the LTV exceeds its maintenance threshold, typically in the 60–70% zone depending on the institution and the collateral. The operating rule is a maximum target LTV of 20%, managed with flows and not with sales: in rising markets the credit capacity grows and admits new draws; in drawdowns, the upward drift of the LTV is contained with periodic contributions and moderated draws — never by liquidating the core.

The 20% cap is not an arbitrary figure: it is the level that survives, with margin, the worst recorded drawdown of the world index over 125 years of data. A higher LTV is possible with specific hedges that bound the portfolio’s maximum drawdown, but that introduces additional models and costs belonging to a more institutional domain, outside the scope of this guide. Forcing the liquidation of a portfolio pledged at 20% would require a global drawdown on the order of 65–70% — a scenario that has occurred in individual countries (the U.S. between 1929 and 1932, Japan since 1989), which is precisely the argument for a global core, but never in the aggregate world index, whose collapse of that magnitude would imply the simultaneous failure of the collateral of every alternative.

6.Advanced strategies: a note

For the investor who, with the above architecture already constituted, seeks more expected compounding per unit of realized risk, or more capital efficiency, there is an additional layer: the systematic management of exposure through index futures, superimposed on a physical core that is not sold. The use of futures allows the market exposure to be modulated without liquidating the core, preserving the tax efficiency of section 3, and the quantitative capability to be applied to what is genuinely manageable systematically —volatility and risk— rather than to trying to beat the index by abandoning it.

These programs —SCGA and SCRA— are developed in detail in our institutional document, which presents their mechanics, their performance attribution, and the access terms, and are addressed to qualified investors. They employ leverage, and the assessment of their fit within a specific wealth is, in every case, a matter for a personal conversation.
